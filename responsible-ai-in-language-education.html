Responsible AI in Language Education: Accuracy, Feedback and Learner Oversight

What does the evidence actually support when AI is used to explain language, correct errors, generate feedback, score work or act as a conversational partner—and what safeguards are needed when a fluent system can also be wrong?

63 sources reviewedSecond-language learning + AI literacy + governanceUpdated August 2026

The defensible position

Evidence
A 2025 systematic review synthesized 30 empirical studies from 2020–2024 and found promising chatbot-supported outcomes across productive and receptive L2 skills, particularly speaking and writing.1 A separate classroom-focused systematic review synthesized 49 empirical GenAI studies and likewise found meaningful instructional roles alongside substantial limitations.2
Claim
Research suggests AI-supported tools can provide additional opportunities for practice and feedback, although outcomes depend on instructional design, learner behaviour and appropriate oversight.
Not supported
“AI has been scientifically proven to teach French” is too broad. The research tests particular tools, prompts, tasks, languages, learners and outcomes—not a universal autonomous “AI teacher.”
Design rule
Use AI to increase practice, noticing, revision and interaction; do not let it silently replace the learner’s attempt, the teacher’s judgment, validated source material or high-stakes assessment procedures.

01 · Executive summary

AI can be useful without being authoritative

The literature supports a deliberately balanced conclusion: language-learning AI is most defensible as a practice-and-feedback layer, not as an unquestionable source of truth.

Systematic reviews and meta-analyses generally report positive effects for chatbot-supported language learning, automated writing evaluation, pronunciation technology and AI-enabled formative assessment.12424344454950 Those effects are real enough to reject the claim that AI has no educational value. At the same time, the same literature repeatedly identifies accuracy problems, shallow or inconsistent feedback, uneven performance across tasks and languages, privacy concerns, academic-integrity problems and learner over-reliance.345152021262730

This creates an important distinction between usefulness and trustworthiness. A system can be useful for generating another practice question, offering a possible correction or keeping a conversation moving even if it is not reliable enough to make unreviewed high-stakes decisions. Responsible design therefore asks two questions separately: Does this feature create useful learning activity? and How much authority should this output be given?

What the literature supports

More opportunities for low-stakes practice, rapid formative feedback, revision, conversation, pronunciation work, idea generation and personalized examples—when the task keeps the learner cognitively active.112164243

What the literature does not justify

Assuming fluent output is correct; delegating final grading or proficiency certification to a general-purpose model; letting the model write instead of the learner; or generalizing English-language validation automatically to French or other languages.131517262734

02 · Review method

A structured evidence review with explicit boundaries

Search approach. Publisher and DOI searches were combined with reference chaining from recent systematic reviews and targeted searches for second language, generative AI, chatbot, automated feedback, hallucination, AI literacy, over-reliance, multilingual bias, assessment, human oversight and related terms. Priority was given to peer-reviewed systematic reviews/meta-analyses, direct L2 studies and major governance frameworks.

Interpretive rule. Positive findings are reported at the level they were tested. A writing-feedback study supports a claim about that feedback context—not a claim that “AI teaches languages.” General-education studies on hallucination or cognitive offloading are used as boundary evidence and are clearly distinguished from direct L2 evidence.

Limitations. GenAI changes faster than normal publication cycles; model versions, prompts and interfaces can materially alter results. Much of the current L2 evidence is concentrated in English and higher education, and many studies remain short-term.2347

03 · What AI can help with

The strongest case is additional practice plus actionable feedback

Across the literature, AI’s clearest educational advantage is not that it “knows everything.” It is that software can make another interaction available at near-zero marginal cost: another conversation turn, another pronunciation attempt, another example, another revision cycle, another comprehension question or another explanation phrased differently.14749

01

Conversation practice

Chatbots can create abundant low-stakes interaction and may reduce the social cost of speaking practice. Reviews report especially promising outcomes for productive skills, while still noting that nuanced human communication is difficult to replicate.167

02

Writing & revision

Automated feedback has a longer evidence base than ChatGPT. Meta-analyses support AWE and computer-mediated feedback, but successful use typically involves revision and learner engagement rather than simple acceptance of corrections.434452535455

03

Pronunciation

ASR can support repeated pronunciation practice, especially when feedback is explicit and learners can retry. The evidence is stronger for defined speech targets than for treating a general chatbot as a pronunciation authority.4248

04

Formative assessment

AI-enabled assessment can help generate rapid formative information and identify possible areas for revision. The evidence does not eliminate the need for validated rubrics, moderation and human review where consequences are substantial.13173445

04 · Accuracy & hallucination

Fluency is not a reliability guarantee

A learner may be least able to verify the output precisely where they need the most help.

Language models produce plausible sequences of language; that property can generate excellent examples and explanations, but it can also generate confident errors. Education reviews repeatedly identify inaccurate or misleading responses as a core implementation problem.42038 In L2 writing, the evidence is mixed rather than simply negative: ChatGPT aligned strongly with human accuracy judgments in one corpus-based study,13 while a teacher-versus-GPT comparison found that GPT’s feedback strategy could vary even when the prompt and learner text were held constant.15

That means “the AI got this exercise right” and “the AI is a reliable language authority” are different propositions. Reliability must be evaluated by target language, task, model/version, prompt, learner level and error type. Grammatical-error-correction research likewise shows that LLM performance changes across benchmarks and correction objectives.2829

Accuracy safeguard

When correctness matters, the product should make it easy to verify against a controlled curriculum, trusted reference, rubric, teacher or validated model. Confidence of wording should never be treated as confidence of fact.

05 · Feedback quality

Feedback helps when it causes the learner to do something better next

Feedback research predates GenAI by decades. The durable principle is that useful feedback should help a learner understand the gap between current and desired performance and support a next action.565758 Generative AI matters because it can make that feedback more immediate and individualized—but speed does not guarantee diagnostic quality.

Recent L2 studies illustrate the range. AI-generated feedback can support revision and motivation under carefully designed conditions.12 ChatGPT-supported teacher feedback can scale individualized comments while retaining teacher adaptation and learner revision.16 Yet learners engage with AI corrective feedback differently, accepting some comments, questioning others and sometimes struggling with interpretation.14 The safest pedagogical model is therefore not “AI corrects the learner”; it is AI offers a feedback move that the learner must process.

06 · Learner & teacher oversight

Oversight should increase with consequence and ambiguity

Human oversight is often discussed as if every AI interaction needs a teacher watching in real time. That is neither necessary nor scalable. A more useful model is risk-proportionate oversight: low-consequence practice can be highly automated, while ambiguous or consequential decisions require progressively stronger human review.6213439

07 · Agency & over-reliance

The learner still needs to retrieve, decide, formulate and revise

The most important responsible-AI problem may not be an occasional incorrect answer. It may be a perfectly polished answer that removes the cognitive work the learner was supposed to perform. SLA theories of output, interaction and noticing all assume that learners actively process language—not merely inspect finished text.596061

Language-specific reviews link AI to autonomy and self-regulation when learners set goals, monitor progress and critically use feedback.9 But general experimental evidence shows the opposite possibility: in one randomized trial, unrestricted ChatGPT-assisted study led to worse delayed retention than traditional study.18 A separate study of deliberately incorrect recommendations found substantial over-reliance and showed that a brief warning about AI limitations did not reliably solve the problem.30

A practical test

After the AI disappears, can the learner perform better on a new sentence, a delayed prompt, an unfamiliar speaker or a human conversation? If not, the tool may have improved task completion without improving the underlying language skill.

08 · Assessment boundaries

Assistance, moderation and final judgment are different roles

AI can be useful in assessment without being allowed to decide the outcome. Studies show that ChatGPT can correlate well with human judgments on defined L2 writing measures,13 and some versions can provide relevant feedback or consistent scores within specific classroom rubrics.17 Meta-analysis also suggests a positive learning role for AI-enabled assessment when it is formative and iterative.45

But these findings do not establish general validity for placement, certification or high-stakes scoring. Version changes, prompt dependence, task specificity and model bias matter. Responsible systems distinguish feedback generation, second-opinion moderation, scoring assistance and final decision authority. Human-in-the-loop marking research explicitly recommends keeping the final judgment subject to oversight.34

09 · Bias, language & equity

Validation in English is not validation in every language—or every variety

Language education makes AI bias unusually consequential because the technology is literally judging language. Multilingual educational evaluations show meaningful performance drops outside English and recommend verifying an LLM in the actual target language and task before deployment.26 Research on English dialects likewise shows that models can treat non-standard varieties differently in comprehension, tone and stereotyping.27

That creates two distinct risks. First, a system may simply perform less accurately in lower-resource languages. Second, it may incorrectly treat a legitimate dialect, accent or pragmatic style as deficient. Responsible feedback systems should therefore distinguish target-language error from variation, disclose the limits of automated judgments and avoid using raw model scores as proxies for intelligence, effort or communicative worth.

Language-resource bias

Models tend to be strongest where training and evaluation data are richest. Validate French, not merely English, and validate the exact learner task.26

Dialect & accent bias

“Standardness” is not the same thing as correctness or communicative success. Feedback needs sociolinguistic guardrails.2742

Access & literacy gaps

AI can expand support for underserved learners, but those with stronger language and AI literacy may also be better able to detect bad output, creating a new layer of inequality.82340

10 · Privacy & governance

Language practice can contain personal data even when the task looks harmless

Learners may submit autobiographical writing, voice recordings, immigration-related material, schoolwork, workplace language or sensitive questions. UNESCO therefore places data privacy, age-appropriate design and human-centred validation at the core of GenAI guidance for education.2122 Responsible products should minimize data collection, explain what is sent to third-party models, separate learning records from model prompts where feasible, and avoid encouraging learners to disclose unnecessary personal information.

Governance also includes transparency. Learners should know when feedback is AI-generated, when a teacher has reviewed it, what the system is intended to assess and what it is not authorized to decide. This becomes especially important when AI output looks polished enough to be mistaken for institutional judgment.323334

11 · Responsible design rules

Make the learner stronger than the tool-supported task

Ten evidence-aligned rules

  1. Attempt before answer. Require learner production before showing a complete AI solution whenever the target skill is production.
  2. Feedback before replacement. Prefer hints, error locations, explanations and guided revision over instant rewriting.5657
  3. Require a second attempt. Feedback that does not trigger revision is often only information.
  4. Verify high-value claims. Ground explanations in controlled curriculum or trusted reference material where possible.2021
  5. Escalate ambiguity. Offer a human or authoritative reference path for contested grammar, pragmatics, assessment or cultural nuance.61639
  6. Validate by language and task. Do not assume an English benchmark proves French performance.2627
  7. Keep consequential assessment human-led. AI may assist scoring or moderation, but validity and accountability cannot be inferred from fluency.173445
  8. Teach calibrated trust. Learners need practice accepting good suggestions and rejecting bad ones; a one-time disclaimer is not enough.2330
  9. Protect learner data. Minimize sensitive prompts, disclose third-party processing and apply age-appropriate safeguards.2122
  10. Measure unaided transfer. Evaluate whether gains remain when the AI is removed, delayed or replaced with a human interaction.1862

Bottom line

Responsible AI is not anti-AI; it is evidence-aligned AI

The current literature supports a position that is neither promotional nor alarmist. Research suggests AI-supported tools can provide additional opportunities for practice and feedback, although outcomes depend on instructional design, learner behaviour and appropriate oversight. The strongest educational use cases keep the learner producing language, evaluating feedback and trying again. The weakest ones confuse fluent generation with truth, convenience with learning, or automated scoring with validated judgment. AI should make high-quality language practice easier to access while leaving epistemic authority, consequential decisions and the learner’s core cognitive work where they belong.
