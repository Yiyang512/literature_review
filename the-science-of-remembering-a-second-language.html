The core answer

Question
What makes a second-language memory durable enough to be recalled days, months, or years later?
Answer
Not repetition alone. The most defensible pattern is repeated successful retrieval across spaced sessions, with corrective feedback and enough variation that the learner can use the knowledge under new cues.
Best evidence
The L2-specific meta-analysis by Kim & Webb synthesized 98 effect sizes from 48 experiments (N=3,411) and found a medium-to-large spacing effect overall; longer spacing was more advantageous on delayed tests, while equal and expanding spacing were statistically equivalent on average.41
Critical caveat
There is no scientifically justified universal interval such as “1–3–7–14 days.” Optimal spacing changes with the intended retention interval, proficiency, age, target knowledge, task and whether the goal is declarative recall or procedural performance.1241434649

01 · Executive synthesis

Durable language memory is built by reconstruction, not by comfortable exposure

Second-language study often feels productive when a word, rule or sentence looks familiar. The memory literature repeatedly shows why that feeling is dangerous: availability during study is not the same as later accessibility without the answer in front of you.112531

Three mechanisms need to be separated. Repetition increases the number of encounters. Retrieval requires the learner to reconstruct a target from a cue. Spacing changes the temporal distance between encounters or retrievals. Repetition is therefore the dosage; retrieval is the memory operation; spacing is the timing policy. The strongest learning architecture uses all three, but it does not assume that more repetition, harder retrieval or longer spacing is always better.791027

Bottom line

Remembering a language is a control problem, not a calendar problem. A good system watches whether the learner can retrieve, how confidently and how recently; it lengthens the gap after success, shortens it after failure, and periodically changes the cue so memory becomes usable rather than merely familiar.

02 · Review method

An evidence-focused integrative review, with deliberate emphasis on delayed retention

This review searched publisher pages, scholarly indexes, bibliographic records and open research materials available on the web through August 7, 2026. Priority was given to (1) quantitative syntheses and systematic reviews of retrieval or spacing, (2) landmark experimental work establishing mechanisms, and (3) peer-reviewed second-language studies that directly manipulated retrieval opportunities, repetition count or practice distribution.

Selection and interpretation rules

Inclusion. A paper had to contribute directly to retrieval practice, repeated encounters, distributed practice, successive relearning, or a language-learning boundary condition relevant to those mechanisms. The final set contains 62 scholarly works, including 30 L2-specific studies.

Outcome hierarchy. Delayed recall/production was treated as stronger evidence of durable memory than immediate posttest performance. Recognition, cued recall, free recall, productive vocabulary, grammar judgement, pronunciation and speech fluency were not treated as interchangeable outcomes.

Figure policy. We did not copy figures from ordinary copyrighted articles. Three figures below come from Cambridge University Press articles explicitly licensed under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0. All other diagrams are author syntheses that summarize patterns without recreating a source figure.

Limit. This is not a preregistered PRISMA systematic review. It is a deep, evidence-focused literature review designed to explain converging mechanisms, conflicting findings and practical implications; the 62-paper matrix makes every included source and review conclusion auditable.

03 · Memory problem

A learner can “know” a French word at 8 p.m. and fail to retrieve it next week

Memory performance depends on the match between what was encoded, the cue available later, and the accessibility of the target at that moment. Repeated exposure can make processing fast and familiar while leaving recall fragile. That is why restudy often performs well immediately but loses to retrieval after a delay.111325

For a second language, this problem multiplies. A lexical item includes orthography, sound, meaning, grammatical behavior, collocations and the ability to retrieve it during comprehension or production. The repetition literature shows that different dimensions of word knowledge develop at different rates; even many encounters do not produce “complete” lexical mastery.555758

04 · Retrieval

The act of trying to remember is itself a learning event

Retrieval practice is not simply measurement. When a learner reconstructs peindre from “to paint,” produces a verb form from a sentence cue, or recalls a phrase after hearing its meaning, the retrieval changes the memory that will be available later.131823

01

Keep retrieving after the first correct answer

In a foreign-vocabulary paradigm, Karpicke & Roediger found that additional study after initial mastery contributed little to delayed recall, whereas repeated testing after mastery produced a large benefit.13

02

Make retrieval effortful—but still successful

More difficult successful retrieval can strengthen later memory, but difficulty that causes repeated failure is not automatically desirable. Cue support should fade as the learner stabilizes.14

03

Use productive recall, not recognition alone

Meta-analytic and classroom evidence indicates that recall/production tests often create larger learning gains than recognition-only tests.1830

04

Give corrective feedback

Feedback protects against reinforcing errors and can improve later recall after testing. In language learning, feedback timing is a moderator rather than a universally fixed rule.294142

L2-specific evidence

Barcroft created a tiny retrieval window by delaying the Spanish target word after the picture. That opportunity to try recall improved immediate, 2-day and 1-week vocabulary performance.37 Folse likewise found that repeated retrieval-oriented exercises could outperform a single more elaborate exercise.59

05 · Repetition

Ten encounters are not ten equivalent learning events

“Repetition” hides several variables: Is the answer visible or generated? Is the word encountered in the same sentence or a new one? Is the learner reading, hearing, writing or speaking? Does the repetition occur ten seconds later or ten days later? Research on word learning shows a dose–response relation, but also that encounter count alone cannot describe what is learned.9555658

Passive re-exposure

  • Useful for establishing form and meaning.
  • Can create familiarity without independent recall.
  • Naturalistic reading/viewing gains are moderated by frequency, proficiency, modality and cognateness.576061

Retrieval repetition

  • Requires reconstructing the target before feedback.
  • Repeated successful recall has stronger delayed-memory evidence than repeated study.1318
  • Can be cued, productive, spoken or written.

Contextual variation

  • Changes the cue structure around the same language item.
  • Helps prevent learning from being bound to one prompt.
  • Transfer is not guaranteed; it weakens as new tasks diverge from practice.20

Skill repetition

  • Speaking-task repetition can reduce planning burden and alter fluency.
  • Immediate fluency components can improve while other components worsen.47
  • Procedural targets can prefer shorter gaps than declarative targets.49

06 · Spacing

Forgetting is not merely the enemy; a little forgetting can make the next retrieval productive

Spacing separates learning events so the next encounter is less supported by short-lived activation. The benefit is not “the longer the better.” If the gap is too short, the learner can coast on residual accessibility; if it is too long, retrieval collapses. The best region shifts outward as the desired retention horizon lengthens.123

The field-specific evidence is substantial. Kim & Webb’s 2022 meta-analysis found a medium-to-large overall L2 spacing effect. Short and long spacing performed similarly on immediate tests, but longer spacing did better on delayed tests. Expanding schedules were not statistically superior to equal schedules, and effects varied by learning target, session count, practice type, activity, feedback and retention interval.41

There is no evidence-based universal “1–3–7–14” schedule

A fixed sequence can be a useful interface default, but the science supports adaptive intervals. The desired retention interval, success on the last retrieval, knowledge type, number of sessions, age and learning context should all influence the next gap.24143444649

07 · Retrieval × spacing

The highest-value combination is successive relearning

Retrieval and spacing solve different problems. Retrieval strengthens access routes; spacing ensures those routes have to be reconstructed rather than merely reactivated from working memory. Successive relearning formalizes the combination: retrieve to a criterion now, then retrieve to criterion again in later sessions.102627

08 · Language targets

Vocabulary has the deepest evidence base; grammar, pronunciation and fluency complicate the rule

VocabularyStrongest evidence. Long-term benefits appear in classic French/Spanish studies, modern meta-analysis, flashcard/fill-in tasks, contextual reading and online replication.33343537414245
GrammarDistributed syntax/grammar instruction can improve delayed outcomes even when immediate tests show little difference.3648
CollocationsDistributed collocation practice produced gains on studied phrases and transfer to unstudied collocations sharing a node.50
Speech skillsSpacing effects are skill-specific: long and short spacing can tie for fluency, while shorter spacing can favor procedural tone production.4649

09 · Boundary conditions

The literature contains real contradictions—and they are informative

A trustworthy review should explain where the headline effect weakens. Several L2 studies show that spacing schedules interact with age, classroom ecology, knowledge type and the kind of performance being trained.

01

Children in authentic classrooms

Rogers & Cheung first found a short 1-day gap better than an 8-day gap, then in a conceptual replication found no significant difference. Both studies caution against importing adult laboratory ratios directly into primary classrooms.4344

02

Procedural knowledge

Li & DeKeyser found a shorter spacing advantage for procedural tonal-production knowledge even while longer spacing showed some benefit for declarative knowledge.49

03

Fluency

Kakitani & Kormos found 1-day and 7-day schedules similarly effective at delayed tests. Suzuki & Hanzawa found that massed repetition improved some pause outcomes immediately but carried costs for other fluency measures.4647

04

Direct counterevidence

Yan & Zhou reported conditions in which massed vocabulary practice was competitive or superior, including delayed receptive outcomes. Spacing is a strong average effect, not an inviolable law.52

05

Pronunciation schedule

Carpenter & Mueller found blocked practice better than interleaving for French pronunciation rules across four experiments, showing that a “desirable difficulty” imported from another domain can reverse for a specific language target.62

What this means for claims

  • Do not advertise a single spacing interval as scientifically optimal for everyone.
  • Do not equate a better immediate test with better long-term retention.
  • Do not equate receptive recognition with productive speaking ability.
  • Do not infer a cognitive or neurological benefit from spacing beyond the learning outcomes actually measured.
  • Measure forgetting, not merely end-of-lesson accuracy.

10 · Evidence-informed practice architecture

Design the review engine around memory state, not fixed calendar dates

The literature can support an adaptive architecture even though it cannot dictate one universal schedule. A practical system should combine (a) initial meaningful encoding, (b) retrieval before restudy, (c) feedback, (d) repeated retrieval to criterion, (e) spacing across sessions, and (f) cue/context variation for transfer.7132027293741

Ten design commitments supported by the review

  1. Require an attempt before revealing the answer. Recognition can be a scaffold, not the endpoint.
  2. Continue retrieval after first success. First correct recall is not durable mastery.13
  3. Use feedback after errors or uncertain recall. Corrective feedback reduces persistence of wrong answers.29
  4. Distribute review across sessions. A single intensive session is efficient for exposure, not necessarily retention.141
  5. Adapt the next interval to performance and retention horizon. Longer desired retention generally justifies longer optimal gaps, but skill type changes the equation.249
  6. Do not overengineer expanding schedules. Equal spacing often performs similarly to expanding spacing.123841
  7. Track productive and receptive knowledge separately. “I recognize it” and “I can say it” are different states.5558
  8. Vary cues after the memory stabilizes. Transfer decreases as practice and final use diverge; varied retrieval should be built intentionally.20
  9. Use different spacing policies for declarative and procedural targets. Vocabulary recall, grammar judgement, pronunciation and fluency do not share one optimum.46474962
  10. Evaluate with delayed tests. Immediate success can conceal later forgetting and can favor massing.113348

Illustrative scheduling defaults can be used in software, but they should be labeled as defaults—not as a universal scientific prescription—and should adapt after each retrieval.

An illustrative adaptive sequence—not a “scientific magic interval”

Session 0: encode + retrieve to criterion → next review: roughly 1 day if fragile → then 3–4 days → 7–10 days → 2–4 weeks → longer maintenance intervals. After a failure, shorten the gap and restore cue support; after easy repeated success, lengthen it. The exact values are interface heuristics constrained by the evidence, not values directly established as universally optimal.2341

12 · Conclusions

The science favors a memory loop, not a repetition counter

The strongest conclusion from this literature is simple but consequential: durable second-language learning is better predicted by what the learner has successfully retrieved across time than by how many times the material has merely been seen.

Repetition matters because language knowledge requires multiple encounters.5556 Retrieval matters because rebuilding the answer strengthens later accessibility and often outperforms restudy.131837 Spacing matters because it prevents every repetition from becoming a near-duplicate while tuning difficulty to the desired retention horizon.1241 The combination—successful recall, feedback, then later relearning—is the most defensible general architecture.1027

But the boundary conditions are equally important. Longer spacing is not always better; expanding schedules are not reliably superior; children and authentic classrooms can behave differently from adult laboratory samples; and procedural pronunciation or fluency targets may respond differently from declarative vocabulary.384344464962 A scientifically credible language-learning system should therefore be adaptive, outcome-specific and tested on delayed transfer—not marketed around a fixed “forgetting curve” schedule.

Practical takeaway

See it once. Retrieve it many times. Space those retrievals. Change the cue. Test it later.

That is the most defensible one-line translation of more than four decades of modern retrieval research and more than four decades of second-language spacing research.
